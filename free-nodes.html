What a free node measurement can and cannot tell you

A node that answers is not the same as a node that works, and a node that works today is not the same as a node that will work tomorrow. Those are three separate claims, and most lists publish only the first.

A node is three separate questions

QuestionWhat answering it provesWhat it leaves open
Does the address resolve and the port accept a connection?The endpoint exists and something is listeningWhether the protocol handshake completes
Does the handshake complete and the tunnel come up?Credentials and cipher match the serverWhether traffic can leave through the exit
Does a request sent through the tunnel come back with a different egress address?The whole path works end to endWhether it will still work in an hour

Most published checks stop at the second row, because a successful handshake is easy to detect. It is also the row that produces the most false positives: an exit can be blocked while the tunnel is perfectly healthy, and the client reports success the whole time.

One vantage point is not a measurement

A single test tells you what one host, on one network, at one moment experienced. Free endpoints are frequently reachable from one region and filtered from another, so a pass from a single probe is close to meaningless as a statement about the node. Testing from two independent exit points and requiring both to complete the request removes the largest class of false positives — at the cost of rejecting some nodes that would have worked for you specifically.

Median latency, not best sample

Ten requests produce ten numbers, and the fastest one is the least informative of them. A node with one fast sample and eight slow ones has a median that reflects the eight. Publishing the median is deliberately unflattering: it is the number that predicts what the connection will feel like, while the minimum predicts only what the network was briefly capable of.

What a pass still does not promise

Three things stay unknown after any measurement. Whether the operator logs traffic — nothing measurable from outside answers that. Whether the node will answer next week — endpoints disappear without notice, and that is normal rather than a fault. And whether the exit is safe to authenticate through: you do not know who operates it, so credentials and payment details do not belong on a public endpoint regardless of how fast it measured.

How the published list is produced

The daily list applies exactly this method: ten HTTPS requests from each of two exit points, a fixed-size download to measure throughput on both sides, and only nodes that passed on both sides are published — with the numbers, including the ones that look bad. The English list is at free v2ray nodes, verified daily; the Chinese version, with per-request evidence for each node, is at 每日免费节点核验.

Once a node is imported, the remaining failure is usually local: the tunnel comes up and the system still sends traffic around it. Confirming the actual egress address is the only way to tell system proxy from TUN capture apart, and it is the first thing to check before blaming the node.
